Your question

A road repair today, payable in thirty days

This is the case that separates real accounting software from a spreadsheet, and the system already has the answer built in: a document carries three different dates, and different screens read different ones.

Incurred
Aug 22
The day the truck was repaired. This is the date the load margin, the settlement and the profit and loss all use. The cost belongs to the trip that caused it, not to the month you happen to pay it.
Due
Sep 21
Thirty-day terms. This is the only date cash flow and accounts payable aging care about. The money is still in the bank today, and the forecast must say so.
Paid
— not yet
Filled in when the check clears. Until then the bill sits in Accounts Payable as something the company owes.

So on the day you enter it

The bill posts debit Repairs expense, credit Accounts Payable. It appears immediately in the load's margin and in the company settlement, because the cost was incurred. It appears in the cash flow forecast dated September 21, not today, because no money has moved. One document, two reports, two dates, no contradiction.

And on the driver settlement

If the repair is driver-caused, it shows on the settlement as a deduction pending approval — visible immediately, not deducted automatically. Your own rule: a chargeback reaches a driver only when it is driver-caused and someone approves it. The company owes the vendor and the driver may owe the company; those are two separate obligations and both are recorded the day it happens.

What must never happen

The bill must not be recorded as a paid expense because it was easier. That understates Accounts Payable, overstates cash, and makes a thirty-day obligation invisible until the vendor calls. If someone chooses Paid on a row where no money left, the row is wrong.

The receipt number is not optional decoration

Every cost stores the vendor's own number — the receipt from the pump, the statement line, the shop's invoice. It is captured per vendor, so the same vendor cannot bill the same number twice, and so any charge on the bank statement can be tied back to a piece of paper. That is what makes the chain hold up when somebody outside the company asks.

Downstream

Where these numbers go next, automatically

  • Cash flow — paid costs reduce the bank on the day they cleared; owed bills appear on their due date. The forecast stops being a guess.
  • Accounts payable aging — every Owed row lands in the vendor's bucket the moment it is saved.
  • Company settlement — the load closes with its real costs already attached, not gathered afterward.
  • Driver settlement — driver pay accrues from short miles; deductions appear as pending until approved.
  • Banking — each cost becomes a match candidate. A $612.40 Love's charge is checked against three costs on one load, not against 11,651 transactions.
  • Per-lane profitability — because every cost carries the load, and every load carries the lane, this board is what finally makes lane margin real instead of theoretical.
